01 — The System

In 1988, the World Customs Organization did something kind of insane.

They sat down and said: let's assign a number to every single thing humans trade across borders. Every product. Every material. Every animal, vegetable, and mineral on Earth. Then let's get every country to agree to use the same numbers.

Somehow — they pulled it off. The result is the Harmonized System. Think of it as the world's biggest product catalog — organized into chapters, headings, and subheadings. Live horses. Uranium ore. Human hair wigs. Avocados. Everything numbered, categorized, internationally agreed upon.

Get the right number? Correct duty rate. Smooth entry. No problems. Get it wrong? Penalties. Delays. A very uncomfortable conversation with Customs.

Simple enough, right? Here's where it gets interesting.

02 — The Problem

The rulebook was written in 1988. The products weren't.

When the Harmonized System launched, a phone made calls. A watch told time. Glasses helped you see. Those were the rules when the categories were built.

Now think about a smartwatch. It tells time. Tracks your heart rate. Receives calls and messages. Measures blood oxygen. Runs apps. Navigates. Pays for things.

Here's the trap: every one of those buckets is duty-free — so the rate tells you nothing. The duty is the least of it. Each answer carries different trade rules, different legal provisions, different marking, reporting, and trade-agreement consequences. Different everything. The classification system hasn't been rewritten since 1988. Every time something new shows up at the border, someone has to figure out which old bucket it falls into — using rules that weren't written with that thing in mind.

That someone is a trade specialist. And this is what their days look like.

Plastic shell. Two optical lenses. A Bluetooth controller. Slide your smartphone in, download an app, and you're somewhere else entirely. No built-in screen. No internal electronics. Just lenses, plastic, and wireless.

Goggles. 9004.90.00.

Not a game console. Not a phone accessory. Goggles — same legal category as safety glasses and ski goggles. 2.5% duty. And once you see the logic, it's completely defensible: the lenses are what make it a VR experience. Remove them and you just have a phone with an app.

The kit gets pulled apart on paper — optical equipment here, radio apparatus there. Together they make a VR gaming system. On paper they're two separate things in two separate chapters.

A reasonable person would never land here without knowing the rules. This is a Tuesday afternoon case.

04 — The Research

So how do you actually figure this out?

You don't guess. There's a specific set of steps. You follow them in order. Every time. No shortcuts. Here's what a real classification opinion looks like from the inside.

01
Read the Section Notes
Before looking at any specific code, you check whether your product is even allowed in this section of the schedule. Some things are explicitly excluded before you start. The VR controller got knocked out here — radio devices are excluded from Chapter 95 by Section Note.
02
Read the Chapter Notes
One level deeper. Every chapter has its own rules about what's in and what's out. One sentence can change the entire classification.
03
Apply the GRI Rules
Six rules. Applied in order. Always. Rule 1: does the heading text describe this thing specifically? If the product fits multiple headings equally — that's where Rule 3 comes in, and things get genuinely complicated.
04
Consult the Explanatory Notes
2,400 pages of official guidance explaining what every heading actually means in practice. Not legally binding. But CBP follows them, so you'd better read them.
05
Search the Ruling Database
CBP publishes every classification decision it has ever made. Thousands of rulings. You search for similar products, read what CBP decided, and figure out whether those decisions help or hurt your case. Recent rulings outrank old ones. Sometimes they contradict each other.
06
Build the Legal Argument
Now you have everything. You write the opinion: here's the product, here are the rules, here's the precedent, here's the code, here's why it holds up. If it's genuinely ambiguous — you say so. Options with honest confidence ratings. Never a guess dressed up as certainty.
